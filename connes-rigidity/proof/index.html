Connes Rigidity Conjecture

What it saysHow the proof works

The proof needs two groups that differ but have the same algebra. A group here is a set of actions that you can combine and undo, and its algebra is built from sums of its actions. Connes's conjecture is about groups of a special kind, called ICC groups with property (T). So both groups must be of that kind as well.

The plan starts from a gap in what the algebra records. Take positions that can be added, and let a second group K shuffle them. By a Fourier transform, the algebra of the combined group records the positions, each with equal weight, and the shuffles. It does not record how the positions add. Suppose that one set of positions has two addition rules, and K shuffles both in the same way. Then the two combined groups have the same algebra.

The model is the carry on the result page. On the 4 pairs (x,y) of bits, one rule adds each part on its own. The other rule also adds xx′ to the second part, as binary addition does. Both rules use the same 4 positions with the same weights, so only the rule differs. The proof needs an infinite version of this, with a shuffling group K that cannot see the carry.

The paper takes K from 4 × 4 tables whose entries are polynomials in one variable t, made of whole numbers. A polynomial is a formula that only adds and multiplies numbers and variables. By a known theorem, such tables form a group with property (T). The paper keeps a part of it in which no action, repeated, ever comes back to "do nothing". The positions are built from lists of 4 polynomials, where every number is reduced to 0 or 1. The carry adds, to a second part, a product of two such lists.

Three checks then remain. The algebras match, because both rules give the same positions, weights and shuffles. The groups differ, because the carry creates an action that returns to "do nothing" after exactly 4 repeats, and the other group has none. Finally, the group without the carry must be ICC and must have property (T).

The ICC check is a count: each action other than "do nothing" has infinitely many conjugates. Property (T) is the hard part. The paper shows that K can move each basic list to every other one, and it proves an estimate that forbids small motion. Then the algebra carries both properties to the second group, by a theorem of Alain Connes and Vaughan Jones. An ICC group has property (T) exactly when its algebra does.

Infinitely many groups come from moving the carry. Shift it by n places in each of the four directions, and the same algebra appears again. A count of a certain kind of element inside each new group recovers n. So the groups all differ, and one algebra belongs to infinitely many groups of this kind.
