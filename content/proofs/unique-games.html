The proof must turn any 3-SAT puzzle, in a fast way, into a unique game. A good puzzle must give a game in which nearly all the rules can hold. A bad puzzle must give a game in which almost none can. The hard part is "nearly all". Earlier methods could only promise that about half the rules hold.

The proof follows the standard plan for such results. It starts from a puzzle that is already known to be hard to approximate: equations modulo 2, each on three switches. Johan Håstad proved in 2001 that telling two kinds apart is NP-hard. In one kind nearly all the equations can hold, and in the other only about half can. The proof then writes each possible answer as a long, redundant table, and it designs a test that compares tables.

A correct answer gives tables that pass the test. The difficult direction is the opposite one. Any tables that pass often must hide a good answer to the equations, and the proof must find it. It does so with Fourier analysis, which splits a table into simple waves and shows which waves carry the passing rate.

The tables are indexed by grids of 0s and 1s. One step of the test adds a simple grid of a special shape to the index. Before 2026, the 2-to-2 theorem of Subhash Khot, Dor Minzer and Muli Safra controlled such steps. But a natural answer changes under a step half of the time, so the test could only promise about half. In the simplest case, the change is a fair coin: 0 or 1 with equal chances.

The new idea changes the random step. The paper builds a special map that hides small random changes, from blocks of quadratic formulas stacked again and again. An honest answer, seen through the map, keeps its value with a chance as close to 1 as needed. Yet any rich enough linear way of reading the table still notices the change, with a chance of at least 1/8.

The two properties do different jobs. The first gives nearly all the rules for good puzzles, and the second keeps the old analysis working. If a labeling passes almost all the tests, Fourier analysis shows that it also passes the older test often. Then the 2-to-2 theorem recovers a good answer to the equations. That cannot happen for bad puzzles, by Håstad's result. OpenAI reports this proof.
