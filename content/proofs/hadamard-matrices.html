The result page describes two results. New Hadamard matrices were found at twelve orders, and OpenAI reports a proof about matrices with a special shape. A matrix is a table of numbers. Both results rest on one tool, the autocorrelation of a row of signs. Shift the row cyclically by some number of places, multiply it entry by entry with the original row, and add the products.

Take the row \((+, +, +, -)\). Shifted by one place it becomes \((-, +, +, +)\). The products of the two rows are \(-, +, +, -\), and they add to 0. Shifts by two and by three places also give 0. So the four cyclic shifts of this row form a Hadamard matrix of order 4, in which every row is the row above, shifted.

The twelve new matrices were announced without a method. A survey that checked them found their structure. Each one comes from four rows of signs of length \(v\), arranged in a fixed pattern called a Goethals–Seidel array. The pattern gives a matrix of order \(4v\). Some have a small extra border. The array works when the autocorrelations of the four rows add up to the right value at every shift.

Searches like this become possible when each row has a symmetry, so that the same sign repeats in a fixed pattern. At order 892, for example, each row has length 223, but only 75 of its signs can be chosen freely. Four rows then have 300 free signs instead of 892. The two smallest new orders, 668 and 716, needed rows with no such symmetry, so all their signs were free.

The second result is about matrices in which each row is the row above, shifted one place. Such a matrix is fixed by its first row, so write that row as a polynomial \(h\) in a variable \(X\), with \(X^n = 1\). A polynomial is a formula that only adds and multiplies numbers and variables. The Hadamard condition becomes one equation, \(h(X)\,h(X^{-1}) = n\). Setting \(X = 1\) shows that the square of the row sum is \(n\). For \((+, +, +, -)\), the sum is 2 and \(n = 4\).

Older work by Richard Turyn showed that the order must be \(4u^2\) with \(u\) odd. The new proof puts roots of 1 in place of parts of \(X\), and it combines the results into one product of ratios. That product has size 1 in every form, so by a theorem of Leopold Kronecker it is itself a root of 1. Arithmetic modulo 2 then forces the product to be exactly 1. One more step finds a sum that must be 0 but is not, unless \(u = 1\). OpenAI reports this proof.
