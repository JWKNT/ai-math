Both proofs give a method that counts the good settings of the switches, and both start with algebra. Treat each switch as a variable that is 0 or 1. Each rule becomes a small formula that is 1 when the rule holds and 0 when it fails. For the rule "\(a\) is on, or \(b\) is off, or \(c\) is on", the formula is \(1 - (1 - a)b(1 - c)\). The product over all rules is 1 for a good setting and 0 for a bad one. So its sum over all settings counts the good ones.

That sum still has \(2^n\) terms for \(n\) switches. It becomes small when the switches split into groups that no rule connects. Then each group can be counted on its own, and the total is the product of the group counts. Split 100 switches into 10 groups of 10, for example. That takes 10 × 1,024, or 10,240, settings, instead of \(2^{100}\).

Real puzzles do not split like that, because rules connect the groups. Both proofs first reduce to puzzles in which each switch appears in only a few rules. Then they find ways to cut the remaining links. The two papers do this in different ways.

The OpenAI paper adds a new variable \(T\). In each rule it replaces every switch by a mix of switches that depends on \(T\). At \(T = 0\) the mix is just the original switch, so the sum is still the count. At \(T = 1, 2, \ldots\), the mixes are chosen so that groups interact only through a few shared sums of switches. Fix the values of those shared sums, and each group can again be counted on its own.

The count at \(T = 0\) then comes from the other points. The sum is a polynomial in \(T\). A polynomial is a formula that only adds and multiplies numbers and variables. Its degree is limited, so a few values fix it. For a polynomial \(H\) of degree at most 2, for example, \(H(0) = 3H(1) - 3H(2) + H(3)\). The paper also uses a few slopes at each point, with care, so that the shared sums stay few.

The Anthropic paper draws the puzzle as a network. Each rule is a node, and a link joins two rules that share a switch. If a cut crosses only a few links, the method tries every setting of those switches and counts each side on its own. Its key step rewrites pairs of links as single links, using cancellations in the algebra, so that each cut crosses fewer of them. Repeated over many levels, this gives work that grows slower than any fixed multiplier for each new switch.
