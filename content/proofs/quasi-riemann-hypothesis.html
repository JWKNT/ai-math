The proof does not search for zeros. It shows that a zero far to the right would leave a mark on a certain sum, and that the sum has no such mark. It also works with a whole family of functions like zeta at once, because its tools mix members of the family. A function is a rule that gives an output for each input. Zeta is one member of this family, which the paper calls L-functions.

The sum comes from arithmetic with the numbers \(a + b\omega\), where \(a\) and \(b\) are whole numbers. Here \(\omega\) is a cube root of 1, other than 1 itself. So \(\omega^3 = 1\) and \(1 + \omega + \omega^2 = 0\). These numbers have their own primes, and Samuel Patterson built a special series over them, called the cubic theta series. The proof averages its terms with weights that pick out one member of the family.

The key is that the same sum has two exact formulas. A symmetry of the cubic theta series gives the first, and it shows directly that the sum is small. Poisson summation gives the second. It rewrites a sum over a grid of points as a sum over waves on that grid. In the second formula, one main term contains 1 divided by the L-function.

A zero of the L-function makes 1 divided by it very large near that zero. If a zero sat at real part \(\beta\), the main term would grow with the length of the sum like a power linked to \(\beta\). The first formula forbids that growth whenever \(\beta\) is above the target line. So the proof shows that 1 divided by each function continues smoothly across that region, and no zero can sit there.

The other terms of the second formula need their own control. Suppose some of them had zeros far to the right. A "zero detector" turns those zeros into long sums over primes that would be unusually large. A tool called the large sieve limits how many such sums can be large at once. This is why the whole family is needed, even for zeta itself.

The proof runs this argument twice. The first pass clears every zero with real part above \(11/12\), or about 0.917. The second pass starts from that, uses two lengths that are no longer equal, and adds a correction at chosen primes. It clears every zero above \(7/8\), which is 0.875. OpenAI reports this proof.
