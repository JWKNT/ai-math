Put \(n\) dots on a sheet of paper. Measure the distance between each pair of dots, and count the pairs that are exactly 1 apart. The question is how large that count can be. Write \(u(n)\) for the largest possible count.

Small cases show the idea. Three dots in an equilateral triangle give 3 pairs. Four dots, as two triangles that share a side, give 5 pairs. Seven dots, as a hexagon with its center, give 12 pairs. Exact answers are known only for small \(n\).

For many dots, start with a square grid with spacing 1. Each dot has 4 neighbors at distance 1: left, right, up and down. Each pair has two dots, so the count is about \(2n\).

Now make the spacing smaller. With spacing \(1/5\), a dot that is 3 steps across and 4 steps up is exactly 1 away. This is the 3-4-5 triangle: \((3/5)^2 + (4/5)^2 = 1\). Each dot now has 12 neighbors at distance 1, as the figure shows.

Other spacings give even more. Spacing \(1/\sqrt{65}\) gives 16 neighbors, and spacing \(1/\sqrt{1105}\) gives 32. Paul Erdős showed that the best grids give about \(n^{1 + \text{small}}\) pairs, where the small part slowly shrinks to 0 as \(n\) grows.

Erdős conjectured that nothing does much better than these grids. In this conjecture, the raised number on \(n\), called the exponent, gets closer and closer to 1 as \(n\) grows. Mathematicians had also proved that the count is at most a fixed number times \(n^{4/3}\). So the true exponent lay somewhere between 1 and \(4/3\).

The exponent makes a large difference for large \(n\). Here \(n^{4/3}\) means \(n\) times the cube root of \(n\). For one million dots, that is 100 million pairs, which is 100 per dot. A grid with a well-chosen spacing gives about 26 per dot.

An OpenAI model disproved the conjecture. For infinitely many \(n\), it builds an arrangement with at least \(n^{1 + \delta}\) pairs, for a fixed number \(\delta\), the Greek letter delta, above 0. Later work reached \(\delta = 0.043\), so the exponent is at least 1.043 for those \(n\).

Here is the idea behind it. A complex number has the form \(a + bi\), where \(i^2 = -1\). It is a point on a flat map, with \(a\) across and \(b\) up. So each grid point is a complex number. Steps of length 1 then come from numbers such as \((3 + 4i)/5\). The new construction uses much larger number systems, which have far more numbers of this kind.

A later OpenAI paper reports that the upper limit on the exponent is also below \(4/3\), by an unstated amount. The exact exponent is still unknown.
