Aaronson–Ambainis Conjecture

Here the input is a long list of bits, each 0 or 1. A program learns about the list by asking for one entry at a time, and each request is a query. A quantum computer can sometimes answer a yes or no question about the list with far fewer queries than an ordinary computer.

The largest known gaps need a promise about the list. For example, if the list is promised to repeat with a fixed period, a quantum computer finds the period with exponentially fewer queries. Scott Aaronson and Andris Ambainis conjectured that without a promise, the gap is never that large. Suppose that a quantum computer uses T queries. On most lists, an ordinary computer should then give nearly the same answers with a fixed power of T queries.

A polynomial is a formula that only adds and multiplies numbers and variables. Its degree is the largest number of variables that one term multiplies together. Write the bits of the list as variables x1,x2,…,xN. After T queries, the chance that a quantum computer says yes is a polynomial in these bits, of degree at most 2T. Because it is a chance, its value is between 0 and 1 on every list.

Two numbers describe such a polynomial p. The influence of a bit is the average, over all lists, of the square of the change in p when that one bit flips. The variance of p is the average of the square of its distance from its own average. For example, take the average of three bits.

p=x1+x2+x33

A flip of any one bit changes p by 1/3, so each bit has influence 1/9. The variance is 1/12.

With N bits instead of 3, each bit has influence 1/N2 and the variance is 1/(4N). For N=100, each influence is only 0.0001, and the variance is also small, 0.0025. For every N, the influence is 16 times the square of the variance. So a polynomial that spreads its work over many bits makes each bit weak, but it also makes its value nearly fixed.

The Aaronson–Ambainis conjecture says that this is true for every polynomial of this kind. Suppose p has degree at most d, values between 0 and 1, and variance at least ε. Then some bit has influence at least (ε/d)C, for a fixed number C, whatever the length of the list. An ordinary computer can query that bit, fix it and repeat. Aaronson and Ambainis showed that this predicts the quantum chance of yes on most lists, with a fixed power of T queries.

A new paper reports a proof with C=7. Before it, the known limits became exponentially smaller as the degree grew. PSI, a company, reports that its research system found the proof, with instances of a non-public Anthropic model. David Turturean of PSI edited the paper and is responsible for its correctness.

The proof is also written in Lean, a computer program that checks each step of a proof. Lean's own checker and two independent ones accept it. The repository says that a reader must still confirm that the Lean statement says the same as the conjecture. Experts at Stanford are reading the proof, and no independent review is complete yet.

As Aaronson and Ambainis expected, a quantum computer needs a promise about its input to beat an ordinary computer by more than a fixed power. This counts queries only. It says nothing about the total time that a computation takes.
