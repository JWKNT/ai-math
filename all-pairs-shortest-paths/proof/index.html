All-Pairs Shortest Paths

What it saysHow the proof works

The cheapest trips come from a product of tables, in which taking the minimum replaces adding. A matrix is a table of numbers. The minimum has no fast tricks, so the proof first moves to ordinary arithmetic. Known reductions, by Virginia Vassilevska Williams and Yinzhan Xu, turn shortest paths with whole-number costs into a question about triangles.

The first step is a problem called Exact Triangle. In a network with costs on the links, it asks which links lie in a triangle whose three costs add up to exactly zero. For example, a triangle with costs 3, −5 and 2 counts, because 3−5+2=0. A fast method for Exact Triangle gives a fast method for shortest paths. Costs here can be negative, which is why a sum of zero can occur. The reductions then split the costs into small parts and use hashing, so that the question becomes one about counting.

The result is a network with three sides: two large sides and one small middle side. For many chosen pairs from the large sides, the question is how many middle points are linked to both. Put a 1 in a table X where a left point links to a middle point. Do the same for the right side in a table Y. Then the entry of XY for a pair is the number of shared middle points.

So the task is to compute chosen entries of a product of thin tables, which are tables with few columns. For example, if a and b share 3 middle points, the entry for that pair is 3. With N points on each large side, the full product has N2 entries, and computing them all takes at least N2 steps. Computing only the chosen entries one by one is also too slow. The new method computes just the chosen entries, in far fewer than N2 steps in all.

It follows a 1982 method of Don Coppersmith for thin products, which rests on an identity of Arnold Schönhage. The authors trace every step and drop the ones that only serve entries nobody asked for. The structure of Schönhage's identity is what makes this possible, and the trick fails for most other fast methods for tables.

The same method also brings 3SUM below n2. For shortest paths, the gain is small but real: about n2.9995 steps instead of n3. Josh Alman and Virginia Vassilevska Williams report the method, and they say that Claude discovered it.
